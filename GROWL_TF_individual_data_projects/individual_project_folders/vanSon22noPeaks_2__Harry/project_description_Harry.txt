ON USING LLMs — AND THE ONE RULE THAT MATTERS

You are very welcome to use ChatGPT, Claude / Claude Code or similar tools while you work, and
we know many of you do (and we use them too, as you have likely noticed). They are good at
explaining an unfamiliar passage, suggesting what a piece of jargon means, or helping you get
unstuck on Python. We are also quite good at recognising when they have been used — and we
think that is fine.

But there is one rule, and for us it is non-negotiable:

    EVERYTHING YOU WRITE DOWN, AND THAT GOES INTO THE GROWL CATALOG, MUST BE VERIFIED BY YOU,
    AS A HUMAN, AGAINST THE PAPER OR THE DATA ITSELF.

Concretely: if you are about to enter in the metadata table that this dataset's initial masses
range from 5 to 150 solar masses, then you must have gone into the data, run it yourself, and
checked line by line that this is actually true — or found the statement in the paper and be
able to say which section it is in. Not "the LLM said so". Not "Maryam's dataset had that".
Not "it seemed reasonable".

Why we are so firm about this: your work is intended to become part of a publication, and to
become a catalog that other researchers will build on without re-checking it. A table full of
plausible-looking but unverified numbers is worse than an empty table, because nobody will ever
think to question it. Language models are confidently wrong in exactly the way that is hardest
to catch — they invent citations, they mix up similar papers by the same authors, and they
produce numbers that appear nowhere in the source.

So in the notebook you will see, for every entry, a "Source" column and a "Confidence" column.
Please use them honestly. Recording "not stated in the paper — I could not find this" is a
genuinely valuable result and costs you nothing. Guessing costs everybody.

And one real example from this very dataset, so you know this is not hypothetical. The paper
does not state its initial mass range directly — it refers you to an earlier paper, which says
10 to 150 solar masses. But when we opened Maryam's file, it contained stars born at 5.06 solar
masses, so the inherited number is wrong for that run. Is it also wrong for yours? We have not
checked — that is your job, and it is a good first thing to look at. That is exactly the kind of
thing we need you to catch, and exactly the kind of thing that usually only gets caught by a
human opening the file.
